## Abstract 

Robotic assistance in healthcare has advanced rapidly, yet the manipulation of medical supplies remains underexplored. Existing object libraries and manipulation benchmarks primarily focus on household items or surgical instruments, leaving the distinctive physical characteristics of clinical nursing supplies largely unrepresented. To address this gap, a case study was conducted in a Preoperative/Postanesthesia Care Unit supply closet to systematically document the materials, packaging architectures, and storage practices associated with commonly used nursing items. The analysis shows that medical supplies exhibit multilayer packaging, high deformability, transparency, and thin geometric features that introduce substantial challenges for robotic perception, grasping, and control. These objects were categorized, their packaging levels quantified, and their manipulation difficulties identified, revealing fundamental differences from rigid household objects typically used in robotics research. The findings motivate the creation of a dedicated medical‑supply object library and provide foundational insights for designing robotic systems that support nursing workflows.

</HighlightedSection>

## Background

### The Nursing Shortage and Robotic Solutions
Rising healthcare demand and severe nursing shortages negatively impact job satisfaction, increase workloads, and compromise patient care quality. Collaborative nursing robots offer a promising way to enhance clinical workflows and health outcomes.

### The Engineering-Nursing Knowledge Gap
While robotic manipulation can significantly reduce clinical workloads, a major barrier exists due to the knowledge gap between engineers and healthcare professionals. Because nursing roles and tasks are diverse, patient-dependent, and constantly evolving, designing a robot that covers every aspect of nursing is exceptionally difficult.
## Why clinical objects are different
Robotics benchmarks are dominated by rigid household objects and surgical instruments. Nursing supply closets present a different manipulation regime: objects are often sterile-wrapped, transparent, thin, flexible, or nested inside several layers of packaging. A robot must recognize not only the item, but also the packaging state and the safe way to acquire it.

## Field study

We documented the contents and storage practices of a Preoperative/Postanesthesia Care Unit supply closet. The study records the materials, packaging architecture, and physical features of commonly used nursing items, then organizes those observations into object categories and manipulation challenges.

## What the library makes visible

The observations point to four recurring sources of difficulty:

- **Layered packaging:** the useful object and the graspable surface are often different things.
- **Dimensionality:** Flat objects, thin edges, small connectors, and narrow openings demand precise perception and control.
- **Deformation:** wrappers, dressings, tape, and tubing change shape during pickup and placement.
- **Transparency:** transparent surfaces and reflective packaging weaken standard visual cues.
- **Multiple Object:** Objects that are normally stored stacked or inside a stack of boxes.

These properties explain why performance on conventional object sets does not directly predict performance in nursing workflows. They also provide a concrete vocabulary for engineers and nurses to discuss what a robot must sense and manipulate.

## From observations to robot benchmarks

The proposed library is intended as a foundation for task-aware evaluation. Objects can be annotated by packaging level, material behavior, transparency, geometric delicacy, and the manipulation actions they invite. This shifts the benchmark question from “Can the robot grasp this shape?” to “Can the robot handle this clinical item in the state in which it is actually used?”

## Outlook

The next step is to connect the object descriptions to nursing tasks such as retrieving, opening, sorting, and handing over supplies. A useful extension would be an interactive object browser with filters for deformability, transparency, packaging layers, and failure modes, alongside short manipulation clips and downloadable annotations. A second valuable figure would be a task-to-object matrix showing which object properties create difficulty at each stage of a nursing workflow.
